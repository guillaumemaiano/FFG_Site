\# **Ireland**



At the Cliffs of Moher I kept lifting the camera, checking the frame, moving a little, and trying again.



It didn't help much.



A photograph gets the cliff and the sea. What it loses is the drop. The rock keeps going down after your eye expects it to stop, birds vanish below the edge, and what looks like a fairly tidy succession of horizontal lines from a distance becomes an enormous wall of sandstone, siltstone and shale when you stand beside it.



Those rocks began forming something like 320 million years ago.[^moher]



{{< image

  src="images/journal/POST_12_01_cliffs_of_moher_reference.jpg"

  alt="The Cliffs of Moher above the Atlantic"

  class="journal-image"

\>}}



We went to Ireland six weeks ago for this sort of thing.



\*The Beaumont Legacy\* takes place in 1932, but almost nothing in its world was made in 1932.



The buildings are older. The harbours are older. Roads have been widened, walls repaired, windows replaced, floors worn down and laid again. A military installation acquires newer guns, newer communications and somebody's ugly little utility building. A cathedral can contain work separated by centuries and somehow remain one building.



If everything I draw looks as though it was constructed especially for the scene, it will be dead.



Ireland offered plenty of opportunities to look at the alternative.



We photographed stone. Quite a lot of stone.



{{< image

  src="images/journal/POST_12_02_architecture_gothic_window_ruin.jpg"

  alt="Stone tracery in a ruined Irish church"

  class="journal-image"

\>}}



There are wonderful things in the large views, obviously, but I kept finding myself pointing the camera at the smaller ones: the shape of an opening, the edge of a column, a floor, the way one piece of masonry meets another.



{{< image

  src="images/journal/POST_12_03_architecture_floor_mosaic.jpg"

  alt="Decorative stone floor detail photographed in Ireland"

  class="journal-image"

\>}}



Some of it has survived because somebody cared for it. Some has survived despite everybody involved. Ruins are useful for a simple reason. Without plaster, furniture and later decoration, parts of the construction become much easier to read: the thickness of the walls, the masonry, the arches, the successive repairs and alterations. Elsewhere, inhabited buildings show the opposite process — centuries of additions, replacements and adaptation.

This is particularly useful for Beaumont. A building standing in 1932 may have been built a hundred, three hundred, or seven hundred years earlier. In the real world, you can see that. The baker near the church has an LED screen displaying croissants and a stone engraved with the year it was built — 1666. The building on the avenue has an advertisement for a brand out of business since the end of the Great War, next to the holes where the tramway's overhead wires were once fixed.



\## **The Atlantic**



Maritime patrimony was one of the principal reasons for going to Ireland in the first place, and by that I mean rather more than photographing attractive boats. We wanted the ports, the forts, the wrecks, the memorials, the machinery around them and the places from which people watched ships leave.



Cobh gave us a great deal of that in one place.



On 11 April 1912, \*Titanic\* called there — Queenstown at the time — before setting out across the Atlantic. 123 passengers joined the ship there.[^cobh-titanic]



Three years later, \*Lusitania\* was torpedoed off the Irish coast. Cobh became one of the centres of the rescue and aftermath.[^cobh-lusitania]



We went to the Old Head of Kinsale to see the memorial and museum.



{{< image

  src="images/journal/POST_12_05_maritime_lusitania_scale_model.jpg"

  alt="Scale model of RMS Lusitania in an Irish maritime display"

  class="journal-image"

\>}}



There is a model of \*Lusitania\*. There are paintings of the sinking. There are photographs, names, objects, the memorial.



{{< image

  src="images/journal/POST_12_04_maritime_lusitania_sinking_painting.jpg"

  alt="Painting depicting the sinking of RMS Lusitania"

  class="journal-image"

\>}}



The ship is gone. What happened is not. We remember the men throwing children out of safety boats to save their own skin. We remember the wealthy getting eulogies while their servants got a line somewhere far further — when the rich didn't make it. Germany claimed that \*Lusitania\* had been built to be armed and was carrying war matériel; both were true, as the museum clearly explains.[^germany-lusitania]



We remember, because Ireland made a point to remember.



Ships do not leave only wrecks. They leave piers, records, cemeteries, companies, habits, stories, buildings whose original purpose has disappeared, and occasionally an argument about what actually happened. Something like that seems infinitely consequential, yet profoundly futile. Few people walk or drive here. Few people remember the dead. Nobody remembers the pride felt by the men who built it as the keel of Mauretania hit the waves.





{{< image

  src="images/journal/POST_12_07_maritime_leon_xiii_mosaic_quilty.jpg"

  alt="Coastal mosaic commemorating the French sailing ship Léon XIII"

  class="journal-image"

\>}}



This is \*Léon XIII\*.



Why is a French sailing ship commemorated beside the coast in County Clare?



In October 1907, \*Léon XIII\* was wrecked at Quilty. Local fishermen went out in currachs after larger rescue attempts had failed and brought members of the French crew ashore.[^leon-xiii]



More than a century later the ship, in little pieces of coloured material, stands on a wall facing the Atlantic.



We remember those who went into the cold, massive waves to save those who lived.



\## **Listening**



Ships are the obvious part of maritime history because they are very large and obligingly photogenic. \_Which is probably why I feel a form of relationship to them.\_



{{< image

  src="images/journal/POST_12_06_maritime_paddy_burke_radio_officer_story.jpg"

  alt="Display telling the story of radio officer Paddy Burke"

  class="journal-image"

\>}}



We came across the story of Paddy Burke.



Burke had been a shipboard radio officer since 1920. In December 1929, he was shipwrecked when the \*Francis Duncan\* sank off the Cornish coast. Five months later, aboard the \*Lucigr\*, the radio room was washed overboard while he happened not to be in it. He took the hint and joined Valentia Radio Station in 1930.



Good career decision.



The display tells of a later wartime incident involving the \*G.H. Jones\*, torpedoed north of the Azores. A crew member managed to send a weak distress call with a hand-cranked radio. Burke heard it at Valentia, shut down the generators so that he could hear the broken signal more clearly, established the ship's position, and the surviving crew were eventually found.



Forty-one men lived.



There is something wonderfully uncinematic about the decisive act being \*\*turning machinery off so a man can listen better\*\*.



Valentia's marine radio station had been operating since 1914 and, during the great liner years of the 1920s, handled enormous volumes of maritime radio traffic.[^valentia]



By Beaumont's period, radio is therefore not some futuristic embellishment I can attach to a ship because it looks good.



It is part of the world.



A liner has radio operators. Somebody ashore is listening. Messages move ahead of ships. Distress calls cross distances the crew cannot. Occasionally the whole system comes down to one man noticing something faint in the noise.



There is true beauty in the invisible.



\## **Things that swim**



The Atlantic isn't just a place for steel and stone.



{{< image

  src="images/journal/POST_12_08_nature_dolphins_at_sea.jpg"

  alt="Dolphins in the Atlantic off the Irish coast"

  class="journal-image"

\>}}



We saw dolphins close enough to abandon whatever else we were doing and watch them instead. The cliffs themselves are full of birds, the coast changes with the tide, and the water contains a very large number of creatures entirely indifferent to our interest in maritime history.



{{< image

  src="images/journal/POST_12_09_nature_sturgeon_aquarium.jpg"

  alt="A sturgeon observed during the Ireland trip"

  class="journal-image"

\>}}



I particularly liked the sturgeon, an animal which appears to have been designed by an engineer who had heard that fish existed but had not been given the drawings. Armour. Ridges. A nose worthy of Cyrano de Bergerac.



It also has a surprisingly literal connection to class privilege. For centuries sturgeon caught in Irish waters were “royal fish”: they belonged to the Crown.



{{< image

  src="images/journal/POST_12_10_nature_steinbeck_ocean_quote.jpg"

  alt="John Steinbeck quotation about the ocean and its unnamed monsters"

  class="journal-image"

\>}}



\> "An ocean without its unnamed monsters would be like a completely dreamless sleep."



Fair enough.



\## **Other industries**



Not everything had to involve a ship. Alcohol was on the programme. This was legitimate research.



The whiskey industry's basic operation can, rather satisfyingly, be explained fairly quickly. Grain becomes spirit. Spirit goes into wood.



Plus, chemistry, climate, time, judgement, storage, blending and generations of accumulated experience. \_Details, really.\_



The same is true of many old industries. Their machinery makes sense. Their craft takes longer. Dingle Gin and Whiskey. We looked at that.



We also had Guinness.

I have no technical conclusions to report.



\## **Back along the road**



From Galway to Cork, we saw many things. There were cliffs and church windows, ship models, memorials, floors, harbours, military works, animals, old cars, bits of machinery, things painted on walls, and details. Details. Details matter.



Like the front of an old car.

\## **Ever-expanding Library**



A reference library assembled only from things you already know you need is not much of a reference library.



Later, the useful photograph is often one you had no idea what to do with.



You need a hinge, remember a wall, need to know what happens to painted metal beside salt water.



And as luck would have it, you snapped a picture.



A building feels too clean. A harbour feels invented. A machine lacks weight. Something is wrong with a colour and you cannot explain why until an old photograph reminds you what the real thing looked like.



Some of the Ireland photographs will probably become paintings, some will become details in \*The Beaumont Legacy\* so altered that nobody could identify where they came from.



A great many will simply sit in the archive until I need them.

[^moher]: Cliffs of Moher Experience, [\*Geology — 320 million years in the making\*]\(https\://www\.cliffsofmoher.ie/unesco-global-geopark/conservation/geology/). The cliff sequence is Carboniferous sandstone, siltstone and shale formed more than 300 million years ago.



[^cobh-titanic]: Cobh Heritage Centre, [\*The Titanic\*]\(https\://cobhheritage.com/explore/the-titanic/). \*Titanic\* called at Queenstown on 11 April 1912; 123 passengers embarked there before the ship continued into the Atlantic.



[^cobh-lusitania]: Cobh Heritage Centre, [\*Lusitania\*]\(https\://cobhheritage.com/explore/lusitania/). \*Lusitania\* was torpedoed off the Old Head of Kinsale on 7 May 1915; rescue boats were launched from the area and survivors were brought to Queenstown.



[^germany-lusitania]: Britain deliberately used the naval blockade to restrict Germany's access to food. The [Deutsches Historisches Museum, \*Seeblockade\*]\(https\://www\.dhm\.de/lemo/kapitel/erster-weltkrieg/kriegsverlauf/seeblockade/\) describes the blockade as a weapon directed against the population and says it became a *Hungerblockade*; its [overview of food supply]\(https\://www\.dhm\.de/lemo/kapitel/erster-weltkrieg/alltagsleben/lebensmittelversorgung\) gives around 750,000 German deaths from undernutrition and its consequences. The [Bundeszentrale für politische Bildung]\(https\://www\.bpb\.de/themen/erster-weltkrieg-weimar/ersterweltkrieg/155303/der-erste-weltkrieg-als-totaler-krieg/\) states explicitly that the British blockade aimed in part at the *Aushungerung der Bevölkerung* — the starvation of the population. Frédéric Guelton's [French military-historical study of economic warfare]\(https\://www\.persee\.fr/doc/rharm_0035-3299_2001_num_225_4_5061\) likewise describes weakening the enemy population physically, including by attacking its food supply. In its [July 1915 note on \*Lusitania\*]\(https\://history\.state\.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1915Supp/d672\), the German government argued that Britain was preventing food from reaching the German population and that Germany had been driven to submarine warfare as a matter of national self-defense. Germany was also correct that \*Lusitania\* had been built for potential conversion into an armed merchant cruiser and that she was carrying ammunition: [U.S. customs records]\(https\://history\.state\.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1914-20v01/d384\) describe built-in bases for 6-inch guns and 5,468 cases of ammunition, while reporting no guns actually mounted aboard on her final voyage. See also Wikipedia, [\*RMS Lusitania\*]\(https\://en\.wikipedia\.org/wiki/RMS_Lusitania\) and [\*Sinking of the RMS Lusitania\*]\(https\://en\.wikipedia\.org/wiki/Sinking_of_the_RMS_Lusitania\).



[^leon-xiii]: Oliver O'Hanlon, [“Valour at sea — An Irishman's Diary on the rescue of the crew of the Léon XIII”]\(https\://www\.irishtimes.com/opinion/valour-at-sea-an-irishman-s-diary-on-the-rescue-of-the-crew-of-the-leon-xiii-1.3819173), \*The Irish Times\*, 11 March 2019. The French three-masted \*Léon XIII\* was wrecked off County Clare in October 1907; Quilty fishermen reached the stranded ship in currachs and rescued thirteen men before the remaining crew were taken off the following day.



[^valentia]: Valentia Island, [\*Valentia Radio\*]\(https\://valentiaisland.ie/history_culture/valentia-radio/). A marine radio station was established at Wireless Point in 1914 and handled very heavy maritime traffic during the transatlantic liner era.
