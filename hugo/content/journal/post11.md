This is La Beaumont in 1932.



Two centuries earlier, there was rather less to see.


## **Born from a crash**



The Beaumonts were bourgeois. Comfortable enough to invest. Not rich enough to survive a disaster untouched.



Then came John Law.



By 1720, Law's system had tied together a note-issuing bank, public debt, colonial trade and the shares of the Compagnie des Indes in one of the great financial experiments of the century. It ended badly. Attempts to support the share price contributed to excessive money creation; the system broke down in 1720 and the resulting liabilities had to be restructured from 1721 onward.



The Beaumonts lose almost everything.



They leave Europe for America with what the crash has not managed to destroy: commercial knowledge, a handful of relationships, and people who are still willing to answer their letters.



That is enough.



With old partners they establish a small trading concern. There is no grand plan for an industrial empire. They buy, sell, arrange, carry, lend, borrow and survive.



Survival comes first.



Growth comes later.



A merchant house in that world could become several things at once. Trade required storage. Storage pulled the firm towards warehouses and property. Trade across distance required ships, agents and credit. Credit created relationships with banks and other merchants. Success created opportunities to take stakes in suppliers, customers and neighbouring businesses.



The original firm could change its legal form, split, merge, acquire companies and create new ones without breaking the continuity of the house itself.



That is how I imagine La Beaumont growing: not as a single corporation somehow preserved intact since the eighteenth century, but as a commercial organism that keeps changing shape.



There is a very real precedent for that kind of evolution. Harvard Business School's Lehman Brothers archive follows a family concern from a general store to commodities brokerage and, eventually, investment banking. The business that survived for generations was not doing the same thing in 1900 that it had done in 1850.



Continuity did not mean immobility.



Neither does Beaumont.



## **A fortune is not a vault**



By the end of the nineteenth century, American business had become very good at creating organisations larger than the fortunes of the people who controlled them.



Railroads helped force the change. A railway spread over hundreds or thousands of miles could not be managed like a shop. It needed accountants, engineers, regional managers, operating rules, reporting structures and a hierarchy capable of coordinating people who would never meet one another.



The techniques escaped the railroads. Banks, manufacturers, department stores and other large organisations adopted them too.



Then ownership itself became more complicated.



Shares could be dispersed among thousands of investors while control remained concentrated in management, in a family, in a holding structure, in a voting arrangement, or in a group of firms linked through finance and directors.



Berle and Means described that separation between ownership and control in *The Modern Corporation and Private Property* in 1932 — the same year in which *The Beaumont Legacy* takes place.



That is La Beaumont.



A ship bearing the **B** does not necessarily mean that a Beaumont owns every rivet personally. A warehouse may belong to one company, the dock beside it to another, the operating company to a third. Beaumont capital may hold a majority position somewhere, a strategic minority somewhere else, and no direct ownership at all in a partner whose business has nevertheless been tied to the group for fifty years.



What matters is the network.



That makes inheritance considerably more interesting than opening a will and discovering who gets the factories.



A Beaumont heir inherits votes, obligations, debts, contracts, allies, enemies, directors, creditors, favours, expectations and people who have been doing business with the family since before anyone currently alive was born.



Some want ownership.



Others want continuity.



The Beaumont-Steenberghe heiress, for example, does not need La Beaumont itself. She already has her own company and her own position. What matters to her is that the next settlement preserves the privileged relationship her company has with the Beaumont network.



A family succession can therefore become a corporate crisis without anyone fighting over a literal pile of gold.



## **1929**



And then the market falls apart.



La Beaumont does not receive a magical exemption from the Great Depression because its name is on large buildings.



Ships can be laid up. Projects can be cancelled. Asset values can collapse. Dividends can disappear. Subsidiaries can fail. Credit can tighten. Debt that looked perfectly manageable during expansion can become dangerous very quickly. (Seriously. Ask the Bros at Lehman. Well, those who were there. Or Bear Stearns. Y'know. Kings of the world, rags to riches, riches to rags...)



Research on industrial firms listed on the New York Stock Exchange between 1928 and 1938 found that companies entering the Depression with more debt and weaker bond ratings suffered financial distress more frequently.



The balance sheet matters.



So do the people around it.



A Columbia study of more than 3,500 public and private firms found that firms with stronger networks of executives and directors before the 1929 crash had higher ten-year survival rates. The effect was particularly strong where access to working capital became difficult.



That has a pleasing resonance with the Beaumont story: the family begins again after Law because it still has relationships... and two centuries later, relationships are still helping the institution survive.



It's not sentiment. You can't call it friendship in the soft sense, though there may well be real friends among those people you golf with.



It's about trust, information, credit, access, reputation, reciprocity.



Someone answers the telephone, rolls a loan, still accepts Beaumont paper.



Someone knows that though the current company is in difficulty, the house behind it has been there for two hundred years. And, truly, it has been in difficulty before. Some bet against it. They're gone.



The lesson of 1720 is not to avoid risk. A company that avoided risk for two centuries would probably have avoided becoming La Beaumont as well.



The lesson is simpler:



**Never let the whole thing depend on one fashionable asset, one financier, one government scheme or one counterparty.**



La Beaumont does not survive because the family is rich.



No.



The family remains rich because La Beaumont survives!



## **Men die. The railway does not particularly care.**



Thomas Edison died in 1931.



The electrical system carried on.



The comparison is useful because every industrial age produces personalities so large that, for a while, the person and the system seem almost inseparable. Edison was one. Today, Musk is an obvious example.



And then, time does what time always does.



The personality becomes history. The infrastructure has work in the morning.



Warren Buffett made the point rather neatly about BNSF:



> “A century from now, BNSF will continue to be a major asset of the country and of Berkshire. You can count on that.”



Railroads receive rather less attention when they work than when they fail. Ports are much the same. So are power networks, warehouses, pipelines, roads and all the other systems whose chief public-relations achievement is that nobody has to think about them.



Owners change. Managers retire. Fortunes... divide. (Well, unless some heirs have accidents. We're not planning on this, right?)



Governments regulate, deregulate and regulate again. The freight still has to move.



For La Beaumont, that is almost the whole trick.



**It has spent two centuries attaching itself to things that will still need doing tomorrow morning.**

These photographs were taken from a professional machine in a café.



The machine has little in common with the Beaumont building. It is the wrong object, the wrong scale and the wrong purpose.



It is also filthy in exactly the right ways.



Look at the metal around the fittings. Dirt gathers in the protected edges. Repeated cleaning changes the exposed surfaces. Curved steel catches distorted reflections. Tubes disappear behind panels and emerge somewhere else. A joint changes the way light behaves. Heat, water, hands and detergent all leave evidence.



A machine in service never looks like the manufacturer's photograph for very long.



That is what I needed.



Not the coffee machine itself.



The evidence of work.

The pipes on top of the Beaumont building are obviously not copied from a coffee machine. They are larger, arranged differently and serving a completely different fictional system.



But the observation travels.



The uneven reflections travel.



The grime around the joins travels.



The difference between exposed metal and protected metal travels.



The sense that liquid has passed through a pipe thousands of times travels.



Reference is much more useful once it stops being something to copy.



## **Two views of the same machine**



The two watercolours show the same Beaumont complex from different angles.







The wider view establishes its place in the city: the water, the docks, the airship, the monumental scale of the building and the machinery around it.



The other view brings us closer. More of the architectural **B** becomes visible, and the roof starts to matter: pipes, metalwork, gantries, the less ceremonial pieces that keep the monumental building alive.



I like the contradiction.



La Beaumont is old enough to have acquired grandeur, yet is also much too busy to become a museum.



The building has been extended. Machinery has been replaced. Pipes have been rerouted. Stone has been cleaned and stained again. One company has swallowed another. A department has moved floors. Some unfortunate engineer has discovered that a valve installed thirty years ago is now behind a wall.



Like the building, like men and women, institutions adapt or die. If they survive, it's because they adapted fast enough.



That is the Beaumont I want on the page: not an immortal family sitting on an immortal fortune, but a very old machine that generations of people have kept running. When a leader signs a deal, they think of the bottom line and the tens of thousands of people who rely on no mistake being made. **Not on whether pundits care.**



The names on the board will change, but our airship still needs somewhere to moor.





[^airship-scale]: The scale is deliberate. The historical *Hindenburg* was about 245 metres long and contained roughly 200,000 m³ of lifting gas. Beaumont's great airships are a different class of machine: hundreds of metres long, helium-filled, and built around the fictional high-strength, low-mass titanium-based alloy **TOTANIUM® — Beaumont Metallurgical Company**. The helium is less fanciful than the structure: the United States had unusually large natural helium supplies and became the world's leading producer; American rigid airships such as the USS *Shenandoah* were already flying with helium in the 1920s.
